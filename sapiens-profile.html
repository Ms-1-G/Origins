Who was Homo sapiens?

Homo sapiens, meaning "wise man", is the modern human species and the only hominin alive today. The species appears in the fossil record from about 300 000 years ago and spread until it occupied every continent on Earth.

The skeleton is gracile, meaning lightly built, with a high rounded braincase, a flat face set beneath the forehead, small even teeth and a bony chin. The brain averages 1350 cm³.

Worldwide distribution is the feature that sets H. sapiens apart from every other hominin, and the examinations have tested it directly. Every other hominin occupied a limited region, so only H. sapiens would work as an index fossil for a future geologist.

The only living hominin

H. neanderthalensis is the species most closely related to H. sapiens, and the two are thought to have shared a common ancestor. Both were alive around 60 000 years ago, and both lived in the Middle East and Europe at the same time.

H. neanderthalensis died out around 40 000 years ago, leaving H. sapiens as the only living hominin species. Several theories account for this, and the examinable version treats it as competition and natural selection rather than as a single cause.

Neanderthal populations were relatively small and often isolated from one another. A small population reduces genetic diversity and variation, increases the chance of random genetic drift, reduces the number of new mutations entering the gene pool, and increases the likelihood of inbreeding. When H. sapiens moved into Neanderthal territory there was competition for limited resources. H. sapiens was better adapted to the environment and had immunity to the diseases of both populations, so H. sapiens survived to reproduce and H. neanderthalensis did not.

Skull features

The H. sapiens skull is built around a large rounded braincase with the face set underneath it rather than in front of it. The features below are the ones the marking keys reward.

1
CraniumHigh, rounded braincase. The skull is widest high up and the back of the skull is rounded. Cranial capacity averages 1350 cm³.
2
Brow ridgesReduced to absent. The bone above the eye sockets is smooth.
3
Sagittal crestNot present.
4
PrognathismThe face is flat and tucked beneath the cranium. The jaws are small and lightly built.
5
Zygomatic archesSlender, and lying close to the skull.
6
Foramen magnum positionPositioned centrally underneath the skull, consistent with habitual bipedalism.
7
ForeheadHigh and vertical, rising steeply above the eyes.
8
ChinPresent. A bony chin projects at the front of the lower jaw. This is unique to H. sapiens.

Dentition

  • Teeth: small and even in size. The canines are the same size as the neighbouring teeth.
  • Dental arcade: parabolic.
  • Jaw: small and lightly built, with a chin.
  • Spacing: the teeth sit in a continuous row with no diastema.

The trend that ends here runs across the whole hominin sequence: canines, molars and teeth generally reduce in size, the diastema is lost, the teeth become more evenly sized, and the face flattens as the jaw becomes smaller.

2The chin is the single most reliable identifier. It belongs to H. sapiens alone.
3For a great ape comparison, give the paired form: the great ape has a prominent brow ridge and a prognathic sloping face, while the human has a reduced brow ridge and a flatter, vertical face.
4Cranial capacity alone identifies a species only in combination with other features. H. neanderthalensis averages 1450 cm³, which is larger, and the keys explain the difference as a larger, longer and more robust cranium.

Locomotion

H. sapiens is habitually bipedal and walks with a striding gait. Every part of the skeleton below the skull carries an adaptation to upright walking, and the full modern configuration was already in place by about 1.8 million years ago.

Adaptations to bipedalism
  • S-shaped spine with a double curve
  • Wedge shaped lumbar vertebrae
  • Short, wide, bowl shaped pelvis
  • Carrying angle of the femur, converging inwards from hip to knee
  • Large medial and lateral condyles, and a knee that locks
  • Transverse and longitudinal arches in the foot
  • Big toe aligned with the other toes
  • Large, robust heel bone
  • Foramen magnum central, underneath the skull
What each one achieves
  • Carries the weight of the upper body over the hips
  • Balances the trunk above the pelvis
  • Supports the internal organs and gives greater stability
  • Brings the knees under the centre of gravity for a striding gait
  • Takes the body weight when standing on one leg
  • Absorb shock and support the whole body weight
  • Gives a firm push off at the end of each step
  • Takes the impact of heel strike
  • Balances the skull directly on the vertebral column

The hand

The hand of H. sapiens is built for manipulation rather than for weight bearing.

  • The thumb is relatively long compared with the fingers.
  • The thumb is fully opposable and can touch each fingertip.
  • The palm is short and broad.
  • The muscles that move the fingers sit in the forearm.

Together these give the precision grip, which allows fine manipulation of small objects, and they make the hand far more manoeuvrable. The precision grip is what makes detailed tool making possible.

★ Exam Focus
1For a spine question the marks are: an S-shaped or double curved spine, wedge shaped lumbar vertebrae, a flexible structure allowing movement between vertebrae, thick vertebrae, and intervertebral discs.
2Pair every feature with its function. A feature on its own usually earns half the available marks.
3For the foot the keys want the two arches, the aligned or non-opposable big toe, and the robust calcaneus. For the hand they want the opposable thumb and the precision grip.
4The carrying angle is the femur converging inwards from hip to knee, and it enables a striding gait rather than swaying from side to side.

Diet

H. sapiens is an omnivore. What the species ate is read from its tools, because the tool kit is the evidence that survives.

  • Weapons for hunting, which means meat eating.
  • Barbed spears and arrowheads attached to wood, which means spear hunting.
  • Harpoons, which means fishing.
  • Blades and scrapers for processing carcasses.

Fishing is worth singling out. A barbed harpoon reaches a food source that no earlier hominin could use, and it is the only entirely new part of the environment opened up by the tool sequence.

The small, even teeth and the light jaw follow from a diet of food that is cut and processed with tools before it is eaten, so the teeth do less of the work than in earlier hominins.

Diet and natural selection: lactase persistence

The clearest examined case of H. sapiens still evolving in response to diet is the ability to digest lactose as an adult.

Most mammals stop producing lactase after weaning. A mutation that keeps the lactase gene switched on into adulthood arose in central Europe around 10 000 years ago, at the time herding and dairying began. Adults carrying it could digest milk, which gave them a nutritional advantage in a dairying population, so they were more likely to survive and reproduce and the allele increased in frequency.

This is a worked example of natural selection acting on a modern human population within the last few thousand years.

★ Exam Focus
1For a diet question on H. sapiens, argue from the tools. Each tool type earns a mark and the lifestyle it implies earns a second.
2Lactase persistence is the examiners’ favourite modern human selection example. Set it out as mutation, then advantage, then differential survival and reproduction, then a change in allele frequency.

Key fossil sites

Modern human remains are abundant and recent, so the examinations use particular sites rather than named type specimens. These are the ones that have appeared.

Abrigo do Lagar Velho, Portugal

The skeleton of a four year old boy, dated to around 25 000 years ago, carefully buried and surrounded by pierced deer teeth and shell ornaments.

It is known as "the hybrid child" because the skull, pelvis and forearms resemble modern humans while the knee and leg are more similar to H. neanderthalensis.

The Israeli specimen

An almost complete hominid skeleton recovered in Israel and dated to approximately 60 000 years old.

Archaeologists could not at first tell whether it belonged to H. neanderthalensis or to modern H. sapiens, because both species were alive at that time and both lived in that region. The classification was made on anatomy. The specimen was ruled H. neanderthalensis.

Red Deer Cave, China

Over thirty fossilised bones, including three partial skulls, two lower jaws, isolated teeth, rib and limb bones, dated at about 11 500 years old.

The Red Deer Cave people are assessed by how closely their cerebral cortex, prognathism, dentition, pelvis and feet resemble those of modern humans.

Madjedbebe, Northern Territory

A rock shelter in Kakadu holding Aboriginal artefacts dated to 65 000 years, including hatchets, tools for seed grinding and ochre crayons used to make pigment.

Juukan Gorge, Western Australia

An Aboriginal rock shelter with flaked stone artefacts and a 4000 year old belt made of plaited hair. Mitochondrial DNA from the belt was associated with the DNA of today's traditional owners.

Dating method

Radiocarbon dating is the method for H. sapiens material, and the examinations give three reasons for choosing it. The remains are organic and contain carbon, they are younger than about 60 000 to 70 000 years, and the method dates the organic material of the fossil itself.

The method uses the decay of carbon-14 to nitrogen. The amount of carbon-14 is fixed at death, and the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 determines the age. The half life is 5730 years.

Relative dating places a fossil in sequence. Stratigraphy studies the rock layers on a site, and by the principle of superposition lower layers are older than the layers above them. A modern human skull found in a much older stratum can be explained by folding or faulting bringing lower strata higher, by erosion collapsing higher strata into lower ones, by animals burrowing bones down, or by deliberate human burial.

★ Exam Focus
1For dating a modern human, name radiocarbon dating and then justify it: the material is organic and carbon based, and it is younger than about 60 000 to 70 000 years, which is the limit of the method.
2Give the working as well as the name. The ratio of carbon-12 to carbon-14 is measured, the half life of carbon-14 is 5730 years, and the actual age is calculated from the ratio.
3For a modern skull in an ancient stratum, four explanations are credited: folding or faulting, erosion and collapse, animal burrowing, and human burial.

Tool culture

The tools of H. sapiens are grouped together as the Upper Palaeolithic, and within it three named cultures follow one another: the Aurignacian, then the Solutrean, then the Magdalenian. All three are built on the blade, a long narrow flake struck from a prepared core.

Two things mark this tool kit out. The range of materials widens to bone, antler, ivory, wood and shell alongside stone, and the tools become specialised, with a different shape for each job rather than one shape doing everything.

  • Blades: long narrow flakes struck in series from one prepared core.
  • Burins: blades with an angled chisel tip, used to engrave and to work bone and antler.
  • Needles: of bone, with an eye.
  • Harpoons: barbed, of antler and bone.
  • Hafted points: spear points and arrowheads bound to wooden shafts.
  • Laurel leaf points: thin bifacial points finished by pressure flaking.

See the Tool Cultures page for the three cultures in detail, the manufacturing techniques and the full sequence.

What the tools tell us

Six uses are credited, each paired with what it implies about the way H. sapiens lived. Learn them as pairs, because a question asks for the use and the lifestyle separately.

The tool
  • Needles for sewing
  • Weapons for hunting
  • Barbed spears and arrowheads attached to wood
  • Harpoons
  • Ornamental pieces and carvings on tools
  • Burin tools used to make other tools
What it implies
  • Wore clothes
  • Meat eating
  • Spear hunting
  • Fishing
  • Made art, figurines and jewellery, told stories, held ritual beliefs
  • Used alternative materials: bone, ivory and antler

A further conclusion comes from the range itself. Early tool sets are generic, with one shape doing every job, while H. sapiens sets show many different types of tool. A variety of tools implies a variety of specialised roles within the community.
