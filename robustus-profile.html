Where the Species Lived

Who was Paranthropus robustus?

Paranthropus robustus is a robust hominin that lived approximately 2.0 to 1.2 million years ago in southern Africa. The name means 'beside human, robust form'. P. robustus lived in open savannah grasslands and woodlands, and is found exclusively at cave sites in southern Africa.

The entire skull is built around processing hard, tough plant food. The sagittal crest, massive zygomatic arches, enormous molars and deep mandible are all adaptations to suit the diet. This is why the species is nicknamed 'nutcracker man'.

P. robustus is an evolutionary dead end, a side branch that became highly specialised and then extinct around 1.2 million years ago, leaving no descendants. It coexisted with early Homo at Swartkrans for hundreds of thousands of years.

Key fossils

  • TM 1517: the type specimen from Kromdraai, discovered in 1938.
  • SK 48: a well-preserved cranium from Swartkrans showing the sagittal crest, dish-shaped face and flared zygomatic arches.
  • DNH 7: a specimen from Drimolen, one of the most complete P. robustus crania known.

1When writing a species name, capitalise the genus and underline the full name, e.g. Paranthropus robustus.
2All Paranthropus species have a sagittal crest.
3Robust refers to thick, heavily built bones throughout the skeleton, particularly the skull and chewing apparatus. Do not confuse robust with large body size, as P. robustus was only approximately 1.0 to 1.2 m tall.
4P. robustus is an evolutionary dead end. It is not a direct ancestor of Homo.
5Do NOT credit this species with a stone tool culture.

Skull features

The skull of P. robustus is the most distinctive of the syllabus species. Every major feature relates to generating and withstanding the forces produced by chewing hard, tough food. The key distinguishing features are listed below.

1
CraniumLow cranial vault. No forehead. The skull slopes away steeply behind a thick, continuous brow ridge. Small frontal lobe.
2
Brow ridgesProminent, continuous and heavy.
3
Sagittal crestPresent and prominent. Larger in males. A defining feature of all Paranthropus species. Anchors the massive temporalis muscles.
4
PrognathismStrong prognathism. Jaws project well forward of the braincase, resulting in a sloping face, but less than australopithecines. Heavy mandible with no chin.
5
Zygomatic archesMassive and widely flared, with a large gap for the temporalis muscle.
6
Foramen magnum positionPositioned further forward than in a great ape but not as central as in Homo, consistent with bipedal posture.
7
Dish-shaped faceBroad and flat. The cheek region is set well forward, positioning the chewing muscles for maximum force on the back teeth. This dish-shaped face is the most recognisable visual feature of the species.

Dentition

  • Molars and premolars: massive, with very thick enamel and a large grinding surface. Premolars are molarised, they look and function like extra molars.
  • Incisors and canines: small relative to the molars. The difference in size between front and back teeth is extreme. Little need to bite or tear, all the work is done by the back teeth.
  • Mandible: extremely deep, thick and robust. No chin. Resists the very large forces generated during chewing.
  • Dental arcade: U-shaped.
  • Tooth wear: heavy pitting and scratching, consistent with abrasive, gritty food.

1All Paranthropus species have a sagittal crest.
2The genus name Paranthropus signals more ape-like features and a more distant relationship to Homo, compared with Australopithecus.

Bipedal

P. robustus was a habitual biped. The lower limb skeleton is fully adapted for bipedal walking, similar to the australopithecines. Some features of the arm suggest retained climbing ability, though P. robustus was primarily ground-dwelling.

P. robustus skeleton reconstruction
Evidence of bipedalism
  • Short, broad, bowl-shaped pelvis similar to Australopithecus
  • Carrying angle: femur angles inward from hip to knee so the feet fall under the centre of gravity, allowing balance and stability during walking
  • Large condyles at the knee for weight-bearing and stability
  • Foot with a well-developed arch for weight-bearing and propulsion
  • Big toe robust and non-opposable for pushing off during striding gait
  • Foramen magnum positioned forward under the skull to allow erect posture
  • Thick, robust limb bones consistent with a ground-dwelling biped
Retained climbing features
  • Arms still relatively long compared to legs
  • Hand bones show a broad thumb tip and anatomy for a precision grip, suggesting dexterous tool use as well as climbing
  • Small body size means less energy is required for climbing
★ Exam Focus
1P. robustus was a habitual biped. Do not describe it as fully terrestrial and bipedal as it possibly retained some climbing ability.
2Key bipedal features to link to function: bowl-shaped pelvis for balance, carrying angle for stable gait, large knee condyles for weight-bearing, foot arch and non-opposable big toe for propulsion.

Diet

The whole skull of P. robustus is built around processing hard, tough, low-quality plant food. Powerful grinding teeth and jaws suggest it ate large amounts of tough vegetation. Tooth wear patterns suggest hard foods such as seeds, nuts and roots were a major part of the diet.

The diet was primarily plant-based, though P. robustus probably also ate insects and some small animals when available.

  • Massive molars with very thick enamel: for grinding hard, abrasive plant material
  • Molarised premolars: premolars function as extra molars, providing a large grinding surface
  • Extremely deep, robust mandible: resists the very large forces generated during prolonged chewing
  • Enormous zygomatic arches and sagittal crest: anchor the massive temporalis and masseter chewing muscles
  • Small incisors and canines: little need for biting or tearing, all the work is done by the back teeth
★ Exam Focus
1The skull of P. robustus is adapted for processing hard food. Every major feature (sagittal crest, zygomatic arches, deep mandible, molarised premolars, thick enamel) links to this.
2When answering exam questions linking skull features to diet, remember to connect each feature to its function. For example, the sagittal crest and zygomatic arches anchor chewing muscles, thick enamel on the teeth resists abrasion, the thick deep mandible resists chewing forces.

Key fossil sites

TM 1517

The type specimen, discovered at Kromdraai, South Africa, in 1938 by Gert Terblanche. Described by Robert Broom, who named the species.

Includes a partial skull, teeth and postcranial bones. Used to establish the defining features of the species including the sagittal crest and robust mandible.

SK 48

A well-preserved cranium from Swartkrans, South Africa. The most complete and well-known P. robustus skull.

The best specimen for demonstrating the sagittal crest, dish-shaped face and massive flared zygomatic arches. The Swartkrans site also contains early Homo fossils and pointed bone tools, confirming that P. robustus coexisted with early Homo.

DNH 7

One of the most complete P. robustus crania known, from Drimolen, South Africa.

Confirms the skull features of the species and extends the known range to the Drimolen cave system.

Coexistence with early Homo at Swartkrans

Fossils of both P. robustus and early Homo are found at Swartkrans in the same deposits.

This confirms that P. robustus coexisted with early members of the genus Homo for hundreds of thousands of years. Pointed bone tools found at Swartkrans can not be attributed to a particular species as both species were present in the same time period.

Dating method

Cave breccia dating

P. robustus fossils come from cave breccia in southern Africa, not from volcanic ash layers or volcanic rocks. Potassium-argon dating cannot be used. Radiocarbon dating cannot be used as the fossils are over 60,000 years old. Dating methods used include palaeomagnetism and faunal correlation, which are both outside the bounds of the course.

★ Exam Focus
1Potassium-argon dating cannot be used for P. robustus as there are no volcanic rocks in the southern African cave sites.
2Radiocarbon dating cannot be used as the fossils are over 60,000 years old.

Tool culture

There is no stone tool culture attributed to Paranthropus robustus. Pointed bone tools found at Swartkrans show wear patterns consistent with digging termite mounds, which would have provided a valuable protein source. However, because early Homo fossils occur at the same site, these tools cannot be attributed to either P. robustus or early Homo spp.

For examination purposes, P. robustus has no recognised tool culture. The first hominin known to manufacture stone tools is Homo habilis, with the Oldowan tool culture beginning approximately 2.6 million years ago.
