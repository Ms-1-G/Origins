Where the Species Lived

Who was Australopithecus afarensis?

Australopithecus afarensis, a gracile species, lived between 3.9 and 2.9 million years ago in East Africa. The name means 'southern ape of the Afar region' of Ethiopia. Over 400 fossil specimens are known, making it one of the best-documented early hominin species.

It is known as the common ancestor of all later hominins, and has been identified as the species most distantly related to Homo sapiens.

The brain of A. afarensis is barely above the size of a chimpanzee's brain (420 cm³ vs ~400 cm³), yet the skeleton shows clear adaptations for walking upright.

Key fossils

  • AL 288-1 'Lucy': approximately 40% complete skeleton, discovered at Hadar, Ethiopia, 3.2 mya.
  • Laetoli footprint trail: fossilised footprints of two or three individuals preserved in volcanic ash, Laetoli, Tanzania, 3.6 mya.

2Bipedalism came before brain enlargement. Brain size is barely above a chimpanzee. This is the most important evolutionary conclusion from this species.

Skull features

The skull of A. afarensis has features of both great apes and later Homo spp. The key distinguishing features are listed below.

1
Low, sloping foreheadBehind a prominent, continuous brow ridge. Indicates small frontal lobe and limited higher cognitive function.
2
Prominent brow ridgeContinuous, heavy brow ridge. A reliable distinguishing feature between Australopithecus and Homo.
3
Sagittal crestNot present in A. afarensis.
4
PrognathismStrong prognathism. Jaws project well forward of the braincase, resulting in a sloping face. No chin.
5
Flared zygomatic archesLarge and widely flared to anchor the temporal muscles. A distinguishing feature of Australopithecus spp.
6
Foramen magnum positionFurther forward than a great ape but not as central as in Homo spp. This is direct evidence of erect posture, the skull balanced on the vertebral column.

Dentition

  • Dental arcade: U-shaped dental arcade, almost rectangular.
  • Canines: Pointed canines and diastema present. Canines are more pointed than Homo spp. but less pointed than great apes.
  • Molars and premolars: Large, broad and heavily enamelled molars, indicating mostly a tough, abrasive plant diet. Uneven tooth sizes.
★ Exam Focus
1Common trap: describing the face as flat or the dental arcade as parabolic. Both features belong to Homo, not A. afarensis.
2For comparison questions, always give both sides. For example: "Australopithecus has a smaller cranial capacity while Homo has a larger cranial capacity."

Bipedal and arboreal

A. afarensis lived both in the trees and on the ground. This is the single most examined idea for this species. The pelvis and carrying angle indicate bipedalism, while the long curved fingers and mobile shoulder joint indicate climbing.

A. afarensis skeleton reconstruction
Evidence of bipedalism
  • Broad, short, bowl-shaped pelvis supports abdominal organs and anchors gluteal muscles
  • Carrying angle: femur angles inward from hip to knee so feet fall under centre of gravity
  • Enlarged medial and lateral condyles at the knee for weight bearing
  • Lumbar curve in the vertebral column brings the trunk over the hips to allow erect posture
  • Foramen magnum positioned more centrally under the skull to allow erect posture
  • Longitudinal arch in the foot for rolling the foot from heel to ball of the foot and an adducted, non-opposable big toe for pushing off during striding gait
  • Laetoli footprint trail shows evidence of a striding bipedal gait with heel strike
Evidence of arboreal life
  • Long arms relative to legs. The long arms for climbing trees and relatively short legs, limiting stride length when on the ground.
  • Long, curved finger and toe bones for gripping branches.
  • Highly mobile shoulder joint with an upward-facing socket and a scapulae set high on a funnel-shaped (conical) rib cage to allow shoulder and arm movement for climbing.
  • Very mobile wrist and ankle joints for climbing.
  • Relatively short legs, limiting stride length.
★ Exam Focus
1State that A. afarensis lived both in the trees and on the ground.
2Link the pelvis and carrying angle to bipedalism on the ground.
3Link the long curved fingers and highly mobile shoulder joint to climbing in trees.

Diet

The large, thick-enamelled molars and the robust jaw indicate a diet of tough, abrasive plant material. Wear patterns and thick enamel point to grinding rather than slicing, consistent with fruits, leaves, seeds, nuts, and tubers, with occasional insects and small vertebrates such as lizards.

  • Thick enamel on molars: withstands abrasive plant material
  • Large, broad molars: grinding rather than slicing or tearing
  • Robust mandible: for chewing and grinding plant material with force

Key fossil sites

Lucy (AL 288-1)

Approximately 40% complete skeleton discovered at Hadar, Ethiopia, 3.2 mya.

The skeleton presents a small skull, more like that of a non-hominin ape, plus evidence of a striding gait that was bipedal and upright, more like that of humans. This combination supports the view that bipedalism preceded increase in brain size.

Laetoli Footprints

Fossilised footprints of two or three individuals preserved in volcanic ash at Laetoli, Tanzania, 3.6 mya.

Records a striding bipedal gait with heel strike and an aligned, non-opposable big toe, confirming upright walking well before stone tool use.

The First Family (AL 333)

Up to 17 individuals of different ages found at a single site at Hadar, Ethiopia.

Used as evidence that A. afarensis lived in social groups. The presence of multiple age groups at one site suggests a communal lifestyle.

AL 444-2

A large adult male cranium from Hadar, Ethiopia.

Used to demonstrate the prognathic face, prominent brow ridge, low forehead, and small braincase characteristic of this species.

Dating method

Potassium-Argon Dating

Used because the fossils are 2.9 – 3.9 million years old, far beyond the range of radiocarbon dating (~60,000 – 70,000 years). Potassium-40 decays to argon-40. The ratio of K-40 to Ar-40 in volcanic rock layers above and below the fossil determines the age. K-40 has a half-life of approximately 1.25 – 1.3 billion years.

★ Exam Focus
1Name the method: potassium-argon dating.
2State why not radiocarbon: carbon-14 only reaches ~60,000–70,000 years; this species is 3–4 million years old.
3State the half-life: potassium-40 has a half-life of 1.25–1.3 billion years.
4State what is dated: volcanic rock layers above and below the fossil, not the fossil itself.

Tool culture

There is no recognised tool culture for Australopithecus afarensis. Australopithecines are thought to have used unmodified sticks and stones picked up from the local environment, which leaves no archaeological trace.
