What a trend is

A trend is a direction of change that runs across the seven species rather than a fact about any one of them. The species pages describe each hominin in its own terms. This page does the comparing.

The syllabus frames the whole of section 8 as hominid evolutionary trends. Relatedness and evolutionary pathways are worked out by comparing the seven species with one another and with the great apes, and the tool cultures are set out as trends "in the changes in manufacturing techniques and the materials used". So the trend is the point, not a summary added at the end.

Six trends run across the sequence, and each has its own tab: brain size, the skull and face, the teeth and jaw, bipedalism, the limbs and hands, and the tools.

How to answer a trend question

Every trend question is worth double marks. State the trend, then state what the trend tells us. An answer that lists trends alone scores at most half.

The examinations have asked for trends in 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023 and 2024, and the structure of the answer is the same every time. The marking keys award one mark for the change and one mark for its consequence, so a pair of statements earns two marks and a single statement earns one.

The second habit worth building is naming the comparison. A trend has two ends, and the marks depend on the examiner being able to see both of them.

  • Across species: the brow ridge of H. sapiens compared with the brow ridge of A. afarensis.
  • Within one animal: the arms of A. afarensis compared with the legs of A. afarensis.
  • Against the great apes: the human foramen magnum compared with that of a chimpanzee.

These three axes answer different questions. Most lost marks come from starting an answer on one axis and finishing it on another.

One direction, with exceptions

Most of the trends run steadily in one direction, and two of them carry an exception that is itself examined.

  • Brain size rises from 420 cm³ to 1450 cm³, and then H. sapiens sits below H. neanderthalensis at 1350 cm³.
  • Prognathism reduces steadily, apart from P. robustus, which is a specialised side branch built for heavy chewing rather than a step on the way to Homo.

Both exceptions make the same point. A trend is a direction across a group of species, not a ladder that every species climbs.

★ Exam Focus
1Give the trend and the consequence. This is the single most common way to lose marks on this topic.
2Say what is being compared with what. Name both ends of the comparison in the answer.
3Use the species names rather than "earlier" and "later". A marker can see a comparison between A. afarensis and H. sapiens; they cannot see one between "early hominins" and "modern humans".
4P. robustus is a side branch. Where a trend runs through the other six species, say so and treat robustus separately.

Brain size increases

Cranial capacity rises more than threefold across the sequence, from an average of 420 cm³ in A. afarensis to 1450 cm³ in H. neanderthalensis.

The increase is uneven. It is slow through the australopithecines and Paranthropus, where the figures stay between 420 and 530 cm³. It steepens sharply with the genus Homo: H. habilis at 600, then H. erectus at 1000, which is a jump of 400 cm³ in a single step and the largest in the whole sequence.

The 530 cm³ for P. robustus is textbook. Every other figure in this table has been printed by SCSA, and 420, 450, 600, 1000, 1350 and 1450 all appear together in the 2023 stimulus table.

The brain also changes in structure, not only in size. Compared with an ape brain, the hominid brain has a more highly convoluted cerebral cortex and a much larger frontal lobe, and in humans an expansion of Broca's area.

What a larger brain allowed

The consequence half of the trend is what earns the second mark, and four are credited.

  • A larger brain can store more complex information.
  • Increasing brain size increased social interaction.
  • It allowed adaptability in unfamiliar environments.
  • It allowed the manufacture of tools.

The relationship runs both ways, and the 2022 key sets out the loop: efficient tools meant a better ability to hunt for meat, the increase in protein provided the energy required to support a larger brain, and a larger brain may in turn have contributed to more cooperation and communication.

The exception: H. sapiens below H. neanderthalensis

H. neanderthalensis averages 1450 cm³ and H. sapiens averages 1350 cm³. The larger figure belongs to the species that is now extinct.

The explanation the marking key wants is anatomical rather than cognitive. The larger figure reflects a larger, longer and more robust cranium, and a bigger brain case, rather than a more complex brain.

So brain size on its own is unreliable evidence for how advanced a species was, and a question that gives a bigger capacity is often testing whether a student will jump to the wrong conclusion.

Note that SCSA has printed four different Neanderthal figures over the decade: 1400, 1450, 1485 and 1500 cm³. All describe the same species. This site uses 1450, from the 2023 table, because that is the only occasion the figure appears beside every other syllabus species.

★ Exam Focus
1Learn the six examined figures in order: 420, 450, 600, 1000, 1350 and 1450 cm³. Always give the units.
2The biggest single jumps are H. habilis to H. erectus, and H. erectus to H. neanderthalensis. Both are around 400 to 450 cm³.
3A larger brain does not mean a more advanced species. For the Neanderthal figure say that it reflects a larger, longer and more robust cranium rather than a more complex brain.
4For the benefit of a larger brain, the credited answers are storing more complex information, increased social interaction, adaptability in unfamiliar environments, and the manufacture of tools.

The face flattens and the braincase rises

Prognathism reduces, the brow ridges reduce, the forehead becomes vertical, the braincase becomes higher and rounder, and a chin appears.

These five changes are one trend, because they are consequences of the same two things happening at once. The braincase grows to hold a larger brain, and the jaws and chewing muscles shrink as food becomes softer and is processed with tools. The face is pulled back underneath a vault that is expanding above it.

The foramen magnum moves forward

The foramen magnum moves from the rear of the skull base in a great ape to a central position underneath the skull in Homo.

This is the single most useful skull feature for bipedalism, because it says where the head sits on the spine. A central opening balances the skull directly on top of the vertebral column, so little muscular effort is needed to hold the head up. A rear opening means the head hangs forward from a spine that is held horizontally.

  • Great apes: to the rear of the base of the skull.
  • A. afarensis: further forward than a great ape, and not yet central.
  • A. africanus and P. robustus: further forward again, still not quite central.
  • H. habilis onwards: central, underneath the skull.

Reduced prognathism works with it. A flatter face puts less weight in front of the joint, which also helps the skull balance on top of the spine.

Teeth reduce and even out

Canines and molars reduce in size, the teeth become more even in size relative to one another, the diastema is lost, and the dental arcade changes from U-shaped to parabolic.

The four changes are examined together, and the 2022 marking key asks for three of them as a single list. Behind all four is a shift to food that needs less tearing and less grinding, helped by tools that cut and process food before it is eaten.

2The dental arcade sequence is U-shaped, then intermediate, then parabolic. A. afarensis is U-shaped and almost rectangular, and H. sapiens is parabolic.

4The chin belongs to H. sapiens alone, which makes it the quickest single identifier in the whole sequence.
5Treat P. robustus separately in any skull or teeth trend. Its massive molars, heavy brow and sagittal crest are specialisations for chewing hard food.

Bipedalism refines

Every species on this page is already bipedal. The trend is the refinement of upright walking, and the loss of the arboreal features that sat alongside it.

A. afarensis walks upright and still climbs. The australopithecines are bipedal and arboreal at the same time, so the trend runs from bipedal and arboreal towards exclusively terrestrial bipedalism. It does not run from arboreal to bipedal.

Arms and legs: two different comparisons

Arms become shorter relative to legs across the sequence, and H. erectus is the break point where the modern proportions appear.

This trend causes more confusion than any other on the page, because two different comparisons use the same words. Keep them apart.

Within one animal

Arms compared with legs in the same skeleton. No hominin has arms longer than its legs. Only the great apes do. This never changes across the whole course, and it is the comparison students get wrong.

Across species

Arm length compared between species. Here there is a trend: the australopithecines and H. habilis have long arms and short legs, and from H. erectus onwards the pattern is long legs and short arms.

Put together: an A. afarensis has long arms and short legs, but its arms are still shorter than its legs. It sits towards the ape end of the range without ever reaching it.

The 2021 marking key credits "longer legs and shorter arms" as an advance of H. erectus over H. habilis, which fixes H. erectus as the point where the shift is complete. Longer legs lengthen the stride and make walking and running over open ground more efficient.

The 2016 multiple choice settles the within-animal comparison: "length of arms greater than length of legs" was offered as a feature P. robustus shares with great apes, and the ratified answer rejected it.

Finger curvature diminishes

Curvature of the finger bones is pronounced in A. afarensis, reduced in A. africanus, slight in H. habilis and absent in H. erectus.

Curved finger bones are a climbing feature. They give a stronger hooked grip on a branch, and they are one of the arboreal features the australopithecines keep alongside their bipedalism.

The value of this feature is comparative. A single retained primitive trait in one species is evidence of ancestry rather than of direction. It is the diminishing series that carries the argument, and that series is evidence of the trend away from arboreal life towards exclusively terrestrial bipedalism.

The series also survives the argument about behaviour. Whether H. habilis still climbed is debated, and the trend works on anatomy alone without needing that question settled.

One caveat. H. naledi is a late species with strongly curved fingers, and the 2022 marking key credits "long, curved fingers" for it while ruling out brachiation. The tidy gradient is a teaching simplification.

The hand becomes a precision instrument

As the hand stops being used for weight bearing and climbing, it changes into a tool for manipulation.

  • The thumb becomes relatively longer compared with the fingers.
  • The thumb becomes fully opposable.
  • The palm becomes short and broad.
  • The muscles that move the fingers relocate to the forearm.

Together these produce the precision grip, which allows fine manipulation of small objects. That grip is what makes deliberate tool manufacture possible, so this trend and the tool culture trend are two views of the same development.

★ Exam Focus
1Name the comparison before answering. Arms against legs within one animal is a different question from arms against another species’ arms.
2The safe sentence for any australopithecine or H. habilis: long arms and short legs, but the arms are still shorter than the legs. Only the great apes have arms as long as or longer than their legs.
3H. erectus is the break point for limb proportions. From there on the pattern is long legs and short arms, and the 2021 key credits that as an advance over H. habilis.
4For finger curvature, describe the anatomy and let the series make the argument. Whether H. habilis still climbed is debated.
5The trend runs from bipedal and arboreal towards exclusively terrestrial bipedalism. Arboreal and bipedal are not opposite ends of one scale, because the australopithecines were both at once.

Tools become more specialised

Three trends run across the four tool cultures: the range of materials widens, the workmanship increases, and the tools become specialised.

This is the one trend the syllabus names directly. Its wording is that trends are seen "in the changes in manufacturing techniques and the materials used" in the tool cultures of H. habilis, H. erectus, H. neanderthalensis and H. sapiens, and that studying the tools gives insight into the evolution of cognitive abilities and lifestyles.

Each trend is examined as a pair. State the change, then state what it tells us about the way hominins lived.

Trend, materials

A greater variety of materials is used, from stone only to bone, antler, ivory and wood.

What it tells us

Hominins used resources more effectively, used a wider variety of resources, and understood how to use their environment better.

Trend, workmanship

A greater number of blows is used, with increased workmanship, more time put into production and greater complexity.

What it tells us

Hominins completed more complex jobs, did finer work, and had time available to create.

Trend, specialisation

Tools became more specialised, tools were used to make other tools, and tools were hafted onto handles and shafts.

What it tells us

Hominins had more specialised roles within their communities.

A fourth way of putting the same point, used in 2024, is that early tool sets are generic, meaning one shape does every job, while later sets show variety, many different types of tool for different jobs. A variety of tools implies a variety of roles.

Tools, the brain and the hand

The tool trend connects to two others on this page. Making a tool requires the precision grip, which is the endpoint of the hand trend, and it requires mental dexterity and problem solving ability, which is the brain trend.

The loop the 2022 key sets out runs like this. Bipedalism freed the hands to carry infants, tools and food. Manufacturing tools requires mental dexterity and problem solving. Tools became more complex in their size, manufacture and use. Efficient tools meant a better ability to hunt for meat. The increase in protein provided the energy required to support a larger brain. A larger brain may have contributed to more cooperation and communication, and speech and language are thought to have evolved as cranial capacity increased.

Reading a phylogenetic tree

A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis about relatedness, not a record of what happened.

Evolution is a theory built on an incomplete fossil record, and the relationships between hominin species are proposed rather than established. That is why the position of a species differs between trees drawn by different researchers, and why the syllabus says evolutionary relationships "can be represented" using phylogenetic trees while adding that constructing them is not required.

Two things follow for an exam answer. Not every species found is a direct ancestor of modern humans, and a species sitting on a side branch is still evidence, because it shares an ancestor with the line that leads to us.

H. neanderthalensis is the species most closely related to H. sapiens, and A. afarensis is the most distantly related. Both pairings have been examined directly.

H. sapiens did not descend from H. neanderthalensis. The two branch from a shared common ancestor, and the 2020 examination names Homo heidelbergensis as that ancestor.

Molecular evidence

Comparing DNA sequences gives a second, independent line of evidence for relatedness, alongside the anatomy.

Mitochondrial DNA has been extracted from H. neanderthalensis fossils and compared with that of modern humans, to determine whether speciation had in fact taken place. It is inherited only from the mother and mutates more readily than nuclear DNA. The number of mutations in a DNA molecule can be correlated with the time elapsed, so the greater the difference between two sequences, the less closely related the two groups are.

Nuclear DNA is inherited from both parents. Recombination makes migration difficult to track with it, so it is used to trace evolutionary relationships between species, while mitochondrial DNA is used to trace migration routes between populations within a species.

Mitochondrial DNA has been used to create phylogenetic trees to show that H. neanderthalensis diverged from modern humans.

★ Exam Focus
1A phylogenetic tree is a hypothesis. Say that relationships are proposed rather than established, and that trees differ between researchers.
2Not every species found is a direct ancestor of modern humans. P. robustus is the clearest example of a side branch.
3H. neanderthalensis is the most closely related species to H. sapiens and A. afarensis is the most distantly related.
4For placing H. neanderthalensis on a tree, the key wants a position after H. erectus but before H. sapiens, justified as more modern than H. erectus and less modern than H. sapiens.
5For mitochondrial DNA give the four steps: inherited only from the mother, mutates more readily than nuclear DNA, the number of mutations correlates with time elapsed, and greater difference means less closely related.

A: Most closely related, H. neanderthalensis. Most distantly related, A. afarensis.
2024 Exam Q36
Q: State where H. neanderthalensis would most likely be placed on the tree diagram. Justify your answer.
A: A position falling after H. erectus but before H. sapiens, because it is considered more modern than H. erectus and less modern than H. sapiens.
2020 Exam Q38
Homo heidelbergensis had a cranial capacity around 1280 cc, closer to the size of modern humans than Homo neanderthalensis. Many researchers believe it is the common ancestor of both.
Q: Redraw the phylogenetic tree showing Homo heidelbergensis as the common ancestor to modern humans and Homo neanderthalensis, and Homo rhodesiensis as more distantly related.
A: Homo rhodesiensis on a different branch above the other two; Homo heidelbergensis on the common ancestor branch; Homo neanderthalensis and Homo sapiens both from the same common ancestor.
2018 Exam Q42
Q: Describe how the study of mitochondrial DNA can be used to support the theory of evolution and the relationship between Homo neanderthalensis and modern humans.
A: mtDNA is inherited only from the mother; mutations occur more readily in mtDNA than in nuclear DNA; the number of mutations within a DNA molecule can be correlated with time elapsed; the greater the diversity in mtDNA the less closely related they are; phylogenetic trees can be constructed to determine when divergence occurred.
2025 Exam Q40
Q: Compare how nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA are used to provide evidence for evolution and migration.
A: Nuclear DNA is found in the nuclei of cells, is inherited from both parents, and because of recombination migration is difficult to track, so it is used to trace evolutionary relationships between species. Mitochondrial DNA is found in the mitochondria, is inherited only from the mother and has a high mutation rate, so it is used to trace migration routes between populations within a species.
