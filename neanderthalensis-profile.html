Where the Species Lived

Who was Homo neanderthalensis?

Homo neanderthalensis lived in Europe and the Middle East from approximately 400 000 to 40 000 years ago. The species is named after the Neander valley in Germany, where the first recognised specimen was found in 1856. It is the closest known relative of Homo sapiens.

The average cranial capacity is 1450 cm³, which is larger than the modern human average of 1350 cm³. A larger brain does not mean a more advanced species.

H. neanderthalensis and H. sapiens overlapped in both time and place. Both were living around 60 000 years ago, and specimens of the two species have been recovered from the same region, which is why a fossil of that age can be difficult to assign to one species or the other. H. neanderthalensis died out around 40 000 years ago, leaving H. sapiens as the only surviving hominin.

It is not a direct ancestor of modern humans, because H. sapiens did not descend from H. neanderthalensis. The two are thought to have shared a common ancestor.

Key fossils

  • Neander valley, Germany: the 1856 discovery that gave the species its name, made up of 16 pieces of a single skeleton.
  • The Israeli specimen: an almost complete skeleton dated to approximately 60 000 years old, which could not at first be assigned to H. neanderthalensis or H. sapiens.
  • 'The hybrid child': a four year old boy from Abrigo do Lagar Velho in Portugal, dated to around 25 000 years ago, with a skull, pelvis and forearms like a modern human but a knee and leg like H. neanderthalensis.

Brain size
Past examiners have used four different cranial capacities for Homo neanderthalensis over the years: 1400, 1450, 1485 and 1500 cm³. Any of them describes the same species. Use 1450 cm³ as it always falls in the accepted range.

Skull features

The skull of H. neanderthalensis is almost always examined as a contrast with H. sapiens, rather than with earlier species. The key distinguishing features are listed below.

1
CraniumLong, low, flattened braincase. No forehead. It recedes and slopes back behind the brow ridge.
2
Brow ridgesThick, heavy and continuous across both eye sockets, heavier than in H. sapiens.
3
Sagittal crestNot present.
4
PrognathismThe face projects forward more than in H. sapiens but is reduced compared to ancestors. The jaws are larger and more robust than in H. sapiens.
5
Zygomatic archesFlared and swept back.
6
Foramen magnum positionPositioned centrally underneath the skull, consistent with habitual bipedalism.
7
Occipital bunA distinct bulge of bone at the back of the skull. The depression under the occipital bun is for the attachment of strong neck muscles.
8
ChinAbsent.

Dentition

  • Teeth: Larger than in H. sapiens.
  • Jaw: Larger and more robust than in H. sapiens, with no chin.
  • Retromolar space: A gap behind the third molars, the wisdom teeth, at the back of the jaw. This is a distinctive Neanderthal feature and is not present in modern humans.
  • Mental foramen: The openings at the front of the mandible, which carry the blood supply, are larger than in H. sapiens.
★ Exam Focus
1Skull questions on this species almost always ask for a comparison with modern humans. Give both sides of every difference, for example "H. neanderthalensis has an occipital bun while H. sapiens does not".
2The occipital bun, the lack of a chin, the lack of a forehead and the heavy brow ridges are the four features rewarded most often. Learn these first.
3The forehead is described in several ways across the papers: lacking a forehead, a receding forehead and a sloping forehead all describe the same feature and all are accepted.
4A heavy brow ridge and a sloping forehead alone do not identify this species, because H. erectus has both. Cranial capacity separates them: about 1000 cm³ against about 1450 cm³.

Bipedal

H. neanderthalensis was a fully committed habitual biped with no retained adaptations for climbing. The distinctive features of the skeleton are its build: short, heavily muscled and far more robust than a modern human of the same height.

Evidence of bipedalism
  • Short, broad, bowl shaped pelvis
  • S shaped double curvature of the vertebral column
  • Carrying angle present, femora converge from hip to knee
  • Large, strong condyles at the knee, giving a locking knee joint
  • Arches in the foot, with a large heel bone
  • Big toe aligned with the other toes and not opposable
  • Foramen magnum positioned centrally under the skull
A robust, powerfully built body
  • Short stature, with short limb bones
  • Robust skeleton, with thick limb bones
  • Large joints in the limbs
  • Wide pelvis
  • Long clavicle, giving wide shoulders
  • Barrel shaped rib cage
  • Bowed femur
  • Large heel bone
★ Exam Focus
1Postcranial questions on this species ask for a comparison with H. sapiens. The short answer is shorter, thicker and more robust, with larger joints.
2Short limbs, thick bones and robust joints are the three points rewarded most often, and they were asked again in 2025 for the knee and leg of the Lagar Velho specimen.
3Cold adaptation is the usual textbook explanation for the short, stocky build, and it is worth understanding. It has never been asked for or rewarded in a marking key, so describe the anatomy first and offer the explanation second.

Diet

H. neanderthalensis was an omnivore with a heavy reliance on meat. The evidence comes from the tool culture rather than from the teeth: the Mousterian includes hafted spear points for hunting large animals, and scrapers and knives for removing meat from bones and preparing hides.

  • Spears and points: hafted to a shaft, allowing large animals to be hunted
  • Scrapers, knives and blades: used to remove meat from animal bones and to prepare animal hides
  • Large teeth and robust jaws: consistent with a demanding diet and with heavy use of the front teeth
  • High energy requirement: a large brain and a heavily muscled, robust body are both metabolically expensive, which is consistent with a diet rich in animal protein
★ Exam Focus
1Diet for this species is examined through the tools, not through the teeth. Answer with the tool type and what it allowed, for example spear points allowed the hunting of large animals.
2The marking keys say no more about Neanderthal diet than that they were meat eaters. Anything more specific is background rather than examinable content.

Key fossil sites

Neander valley, Germany

In 1856, workmen in a cave in the Neander valley in Germany discovered a set of bones. The find was unusual because it consisted of 16 pieces of the same skeleton, where normally only small fragments are preserved.

This specimen gave the species its name. It established H. neanderthalensis as a distinct hominin and remains the type material for the species.

The Israeli specimen

An almost complete hominid skeleton recovered in Israel and dated to approximately 60 000 years old.

Archaeologists could not at first tell whether it belonged to H. neanderthalensis or to modern H. sapiens, because both species were alive at that time and both lived in that region. The classification had to be made on anatomy, using skull and postcranial features rather than age or location.

'The hybrid child'

In 1998, archaeologists discovered the skeleton of a four year old boy at Abrigo do Lagar Velho in Portugal, dated to around 25 000 years ago. The specimen had been carefully buried and was surrounded by pierced deer teeth and shell ornaments.

It is known as the hybrid child because the skull, pelvis and forearms resemble modern humans while the knee and leg are more similar to H. neanderthalensis.

Mitochondrial DNA evidence

Mitochondrial DNA has been extracted from H. neanderthalensis fossils and compared with that of modern humans, to determine whether speciation had in fact taken place.

Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the mother and mutates more readily than nuclear DNA. The number of mutations in a DNA molecule can be correlated with the time elapsed, so the greater the difference between two mitochondrial sequences, the less closely related the two groups are. Mitochondrial DNA has been used to create phylogenetic trees to show that H. neanderthalensis diverged from modern humans.

Dating method

Dating this species is genuinely awkward, and the examiners have accepted more than one answer. H. neanderthalensis spans roughly 400 000 to 40 000 years ago, which straddles the limits of both absolute methods on the course.

Radiocarbon dating uses the decay of carbon-14 to nitrogen. The amount of carbon-14 is fixed at death, and the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 determines the age. The half life is 5730 years, and organic material is dated. Its upper limit is about 60 000 to 70 000 years, so it works for the later part of the Neanderthal range but not the earlier part.

Potassium-argon dating uses the decay of potassium-40 to argon-40. The ratio of K-40 to Ar-40 determines the age, the half life is 1.25 to 1.3 billion years, and volcanic rock is dated rather than the fossil itself. It can only be used on material older than about 100 000 years, because until then there is not enough Ar-40 to measure.

Relative dating is therefore often used alongside them. Stratigraphy studies the rock layers on a site. By the principle of superposition, lower layers are older than the layers above them, so a fossil found lower than a stone tool is older than that tool.

★ Exam Focus
1For a Neanderthal fossil, marking keys have accepted both radiocarbon and potassium-argon dating. Whichever you choose, justify it against the age of the specimen: radiocarbon works up to about 60 000 to 70 000 years, potassium-argon only beyond about 100 000 years.
2For a relative date, name stratigraphy and then state the principle of superposition. Say that lower layers are older, so a fossil below an artefact is older than that artefact.
3Say what is being dated. Radiocarbon dates the organic material of the fossil itself. Potassium-argon dates the volcanic rock above and below it.

Tool culture

Homo neanderthalensis is associated with the Mousterian tool culture. The Mousterian follows the Acheulean of H. erectus and precedes the Upper Palaeolithic cultures of H. sapiens.

Mousterian tools were made by the Levallois method, also called the prepared core technique. A large core or block of stone is first shaped, then a large oval flake is struck from the prepared core by percussion. The edges of that flake are then trimmed by flaking off small pieces around its outline, and finished by pressure flaking. The important difference from the Acheulean is that the flake, not the core, is the finished tool, and its shape is determined before it is struck off.

The key tools

  • Flake tools: flakes with reworked or sharpened edges, the signature Mousterian product
  • Scrapers: used to prepare animal hides
  • Knives and blades: used to remove meat from animal bones
  • Spear points: hafted to a wooden shaft, allowing larger animals to be hunted
  • Burins: used to engrave and carve materials, and to produce other tools
  • Hand axes: retained from the earlier tradition, used for chopping meat and for making other tools

A wider range of materials was used than in earlier cultures, including bone, ivory, antler, teeth and shell as well as stone. Some tools were hafted, meaning fitted with a handle or a shaft.

What the tools tell us about the way they lived

  • Scrapers, knives, blades and hand axes: able to prepare animal hides for clothing, build shelters, and remove meat from animal bones
  • Burin tools: able to engrave and carve materials, and to produce other tools
  • Spears and points hafted to a shaft: able to hunt larger animals
  • A wider range of materials: able to make more specialised tools, and to have more specialised roles
★ Exam Focus
1The tool culture of H. neanderthalensis is the Mousterian. This is the single most frequently rewarded fact about the species, appearing in five different years.
2Do not pair Neanderthals with the Aurignacian. That pairing has been offered as a multiple choice option and marked wrong. The Aurignacian, Solutrean and Magdalenian belong to H. sapiens.
3Name the Levallois or prepared core method when describing manufacture, and say that a large flake is struck from a prepared core and then reworked around its edges.
4Questions about lifestyle must pair each tool with what it allowed. A bare list of tool types never earns full marks. Write "spear points hafted to a shaft, so they were able to hunt larger animals".
5Learn the sequence of tool cultures: pebble tools, bifacial hand axes, flake tools, blades. That is Oldowan, Acheulean, Mousterian, Upper Palaeolithic.
