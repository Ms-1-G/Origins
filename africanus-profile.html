Who was Australopithecus africanus?

Australopithecus africanus is a gracile species that lived between 3.3 and 2.1 million years ago in southern Africa. The name means 'southern ape of Africa'.

A. africanus is generally considered a descendant of A. afarensis. A. africanus is considered more closely related to Homo than Paranthropus is.

Evolution is a theory and based on an incomplete fossil record. These relationships are represented in phylogenetic trees. As the relatedness of species are hypothesised, rather than being established facts, the position of A. africanus differs between phylogenetic trees. Some place A. africanus as an ancestor to both Homo and P. robustus and other phylogenetic trees show A. africanus as only an ancestor to Homo. What we do know is that not all species found are direct ancestors to modern Homo sapiens.

The average cranial capacity is 450 cm³, a small increase from A. afarensis (420 cm³), but still far below the Homo range. The skull shows a less prognathic face, a more rounded jaw, and smaller canines than A. afarensis.

Key fossils

  • 'Taung Child': the type specimen, a juvenile skull and natural endocast discovered at Taung, South Africa. The first hominin fossil described from Africa.
  • 'Mrs Ples': the best-preserved adult cranium, from Sterkfontein. Shows the rounded braincase, reduced brow ridge and absence of a sagittal crest.
  • STS 14: a partial pelvis and spine from Sterkfontein, confirming bipedalism.

Skull features

The skull of A. africanus is more advanced than A. afarensis but retains australopithecine features. The key distinguishing features are listed below.

1
CraniumSlightly arched forehead and a rounded braincase, slightly higher and more rounded than in A. afarensis.
2
Brow ridgesReduced. Less prominent than in A. afarensis, but still present.
3
Sagittal crestNot present in A. africanus.
4
PrognathismLarge, prognathic jaw. Jaws still project forward but less so than in A. afarensis.
5
Zygomatic archesPresent and flared, smaller than A. afarensis. Chewing muscles are large but not extreme.
6
Foramen magnum positionPositioned further forward than in A. afarensis, but not quite central.

Dentition

  • Dental arcade: Between U-shaped and parabolic.
  • Canines: Shorter, smaller and less pointed than A. afarensis. No diastema present.
  • Molars and premolars: Large with thick enamel, but more even in size than in A. afarensis. Indicating a more varied, mainly plant-based diet.

2Common trap: treating A. africanus as simply a later A. afarensis. Always state the differences: slightly larger brain, less prognathic face, more rounded jaw, more even teeth.

Bipedal and arboreal

A. africanus was a confirmed biped but had not fully left the trees behind. Key bipedal evidence is preserved in the STS 14 specimen (partial pelvis and spine from Sterkfontein).

STS 14: partial pelvis, sacrum, ribs and lumbar vertebrae, Sterkfontein
Evidence of bipedalism
  • Short, broad, bowl-shaped pelvis, preserved in the STS 14 specimen
  • Carrying angle: femur angles inward from hip to knee so feet fall under centre of gravity
  • Modification of hip, knee and ankle for bipedal walking
  • Enlarged medial and lateral condyles at the knee for weight-bearing and stability, part of the shift towards a knee joint that locks
  • Lumbar curve in the vertebral column to allow erect posture
  • Foramen magnum set forward under the skull to allow erect posture
  • Foot with well-developed arch and a robust, non-opposable big toe (with slight divergence) for striding gait
Evidence of arboreal life
  • Long arms relative to legs, though arms are not longer than legs
  • Long fingers with some curvature, robust and strongly muscled
  • Mobile shoulder and wrist joints for climbing
  • Funnel-shaped rib cage for arm and shoulder movement
  • Small body size means less energy is required for climbing
★ Exam Focus
1State that A. africanus was a confirmed biped that retained climbing ability.
2Link the bowl-shaped pelvis and lumbar curve to bipedalism, and long curved finger bones and mobile shoulder and wrist joints to arboreal climbing.

Diet

The teeth indicate a varied, mainly plant-based diet of fruits, leaves, seeds, roots and tubers. and some small animal food is likely.

  • Thick enamel on molars: withstands abrasive plant material
  • Large molars, more even in size than A. afarensis: grinding a varied diet
  • Robust mandible: for chewing and grinding plant material with force

Key fossil sites

'Taung Child'

The type specimen, a juvenile skull and natural endocast discovered at Taung, South Africa, in 1924 by Raymond Dart.

The first hominin fossil described from Africa. Dart's 1925 description claimed human ancestry lay in Africa, not Asia or Europe. This led to the Out of Africa Theory, which is now the accepted theory in human evolution. The skull carries marks from a large bird of prey, evidence that early hominins were prey.

'Mrs Ples'

The best-preserved adult cranium, from Sterkfontein, South Africa.

Used to identify the rounded braincase, the reduced brow ridge, and the absence of a sagittal crest, the key features separating A. africanus from P. robustus.

STS 14

A partial pelvis and spine from Sterkfontein, South Africa.

STS 14 includes a partial pelvis and the lumbar vertebrae of the spine. The bowl-shaped pelvis and the lumbar curve of the vertebral column both confirm that A. africanus walked upright.

Makapansgat & Gladysvale

Additional fossil sites in South Africa yielding A. africanus specimens.

This confirms that the distribution of this species is exclusively in southern Africa.

Early hominins as prey

Most A. africanus fossils come from cave deposits where bones accumulated after being dragged there by predators. Many bones show damage consistent with predation by large cats, hyenas and birds of prey.

The 'Taung Child' skull carries marks matching the talons of a large crowned eagle, which would have snatched the infant and carried it to a feeding site. This is direct evidence that early hominins were prey animals, not just hunters. They were not at the top of the food chain.

Dating method

Dating, a special challenge

A. africanus fossils come from cave breccia in South Africa, not from volcanic ash layers. This means potassium-argon dating cannot be used. Radiocarbon dating cannot be used as the fossils are over 60,000 years old. Methods used include palaeomagnetism and faunal correlation.

Tool culture

There is no recognised tool culture for Australopithecus africanus. Australopithecines are thought to have used unmodified sticks and stones picked up from the local environment, which leaves no archaeological trace.

Stone tools do occur at Sterkfontein, but in younger deposits than the A. africanus remains, and they are attributed to early Homo.
