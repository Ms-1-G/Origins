Where the Species Lived

Who was Homo erectus?

Homo erectus, meaning 'upright man', lived from approximately 1.9 million years ago until about 110 000 years ago. It is the longest surviving hominin species known, persisting for well over 1.5 million years.

H. erectus was the first hominin to leave Africa. Fossils have been recovered from Africa, from Asia as far east as Java, and from the margins of Europe. This wide geographic range is the feature that most clearly separates H. erectus from every species that came before it.

Brain size increased markedly, averaging 1000 cm³, roughly double that of H. habilis. The body is essentially modern in its proportions: tall, with long legs, short arms and a narrow pelvis, built for efficient long distance walking in open country. The skull, however, remains clearly primitive, being long and low with thick bone, a heavy continuous brow ridge, a sloping forehead and no chin.

H. erectus overlapped in time with H. habilis in East Africa, so the two species coexisted rather than one simply replacing the other. It is associated with the Acheulean tool culture and with the earliest widely accepted evidence for the controlled use of fire.

Key fossils

  • KNM-WT 15000 'Turkana Boy': a nearly complete skeleton of an adolescent male from Nariokotome, West Turkana, Kenya, discovered in 1984 and dated to about 1.6 million years ago.
  • Trinil 2 'Java Man': the type specimen, found in Java, Indonesia, by Eugène Dubois in 1891. The first fossil hominin discovered outside Europe.
  • Zhoukoudian 'Peking Man': a large assemblage from a cave near Beijing, China, associated with ash and burnt bone.
  • Dmanisi crania: from Georgia, dated to about 1.8 million years ago, the earliest hominin fossils known outside Africa.
★ Exam Focus
1When writing a species name, capitalise the genus and underline the full name, e.g. Homo erectus.
2Know the average brain size: 1000 cm³. Roughly double H. habilis at 600 cm³, and well below H. neanderthalensis at 1500 cm³.
3H. erectus is the first hominin to leave Africa, and the only syllabus species found in Africa, Asia and Europe.
4H. erectus coexisted with H. habilis. Do not assume hominin species evolved one after another without overlap.
5On a phylogenetic tree, H. erectus sits after H. habilis and before both H. neanderthalensis and H. sapiens.
★ Past Exams
2021 Exam Q37(c)
Q: Dated fossils of Homo habilis and Homo erectus indicate that they were both present on Earth at the same time. List two anatomical differences between the two species.
A: Any two of: H. erectus had a larger brain (1000 cm³ compared with 600 cm³), a taller or larger body, a flatter face with less prominent cheekbones and large brow ridges, a more sloping forehead, a shorter and more rounded dental arcade, a shorter more rounded jaw, smaller teeth or molars, more human-like proportions such as longer legs and shorter arms.

Skull features

The skull of H. erectus is long, low and heavily built. It carries a much larger brain than H. habilis, yet it keeps a strongly archaic appearance. The combination of a modern body with an archaic head is the most useful way to summarise this species.

1
Long, low braincaseCranial capacity 750 to 1250 cm³, averaging 1000 cm³. The braincase is elongated front to back and low in profile rather than high and domed, and the cranial bone is notably thick.
2
Prominent brow ridgeThick and continuous across both eye sockets, forming a supraorbital torus. Much heavier than in H. habilis and absent in H. sapiens.
3
Sloping foreheadLow and receding behind the brow ridge, showing limited frontal lobe development compared with later Homo.
4
Sagittal keelA low thickening of bone along the midline of the skull. This is a keel, not a crest, so it is not an attachment site for chewing muscles and must not be confused with the sagittal crest of Paranthropus.
5
Occipital torusA ridge of bone across the sharply angled back of the skull, giving attachment to strong neck muscles that support a heavy face.
6
Reduced prognathismThe face is flatter than in H. habilis, with less prominent cheekbones. Prognathism is reduced, not absent.
7
No chinThe mandible is robust and recedes at the front. A chin appears only in H. sapiens.
8
Foramen magnum positionPositioned centrally underneath the skull, confirming habitual bipedalism.

Dentition

  • Teeth: Smaller than H. habilis, often described as modern in style, reflecting a softer, higher quality diet.
  • Dental arcade: Shorter and more rounded, closer to the parabolic modern human arch.
  • Canines: Small and level with the other teeth. No diastema.
  • Jaw: Robust and thick, shorter and more rounded than H. habilis, with no chin.
★ Exam Focus
1The sagittal keel of H. erectus is not a sagittal crest. A sagittal crest is a Paranthropus feature that anchors large chewing muscles. Confusing the two is the most common error on this species.
2H. erectus has a prominent brow ridge and a sloping forehead. Do not describe it as flat faced with no brow ridge, those are H. sapiens features.
3A heavy brow ridge and sloping forehead alone do not identify H. erectus, because H. neanderthalensis has both. Cranial capacity separates them: about 1000 cm³ against about 1500 cm³.
4Prognathism in H. erectus is reduced, not absent. Reduced is the word the marking keys use.

Bipedal

H. erectus was a fully committed habitual biped with no retained adaptations for climbing. From the neck down the skeleton is close enough to modern that an isolated limb bone can be difficult to distinguish from that of a modern human. The body was built for efficient walking and running over long distances in open savannah, which is consistent with the wide geographic spread of the species.

Evidence of bipedalism
  • Short, broad, bowl shaped pelvis aligned with the vertebral column
  • S shaped double curvature of the vertebral column
  • Carrying angle present, femora converge from hip to knee
  • Knee joint locks, with large medial and lateral condyles
  • Longitudinal and transverse arches in the foot
  • Big toe aligned with the other toes and not opposable
  • Foramen magnum positioned centrally under the skull
Modern body proportions
  • Long legs and short arms, the modern human pattern
  • Height 1.45 to 1.85 m, much taller than any earlier hominin
  • Narrow hips and a barrel shaped rib cage
  • A barrel rib cage indicates a smaller gut and a higher quality diet
  • Straight finger bones with no curvature
No longer present
  • The relatively long arms of H. habilis and the australopithecines
  • Curved finger bones, so climbing ability is lost
  • The funnel shaped rib cage of the australopithecines
★ Exam Focus
1H. erectus is simply bipedal. Unlike the australopithecines it is never described as bipedal and arboreal.
2Learn the three way comparison of pelvis, knee and toes between the ancestral condition and the fully bipedal condition, since questions often set this at about 1.8 million years ago.
3Accepted advantages of bipedalism: frees the hands to carry infants, tools or food, frees the hands to pick vegetation, allows efficient travel over large distances of open ground, and allows the individual to see above vegetation and spot predators.

Diet

H. erectus was an omnivore with a diet of significantly higher quality than that of earlier hominins. Meat became a regular rather than an occasional component, and the controlled use of fire allowed food to be cooked.

  • Meat and marrow: Acheulean handaxes and cleavers made butchering large animals practical, so meat was obtained by both scavenging and hunting
  • Cooking: softens food, kills parasites and makes energy easier to absorb
  • Plant material: fruits, tubers, roots, nuts and seeds continued to be eaten, with digging tools giving access to underground storage organs
  • Smaller teeth and jaws: softer, cooked, higher quality food no longer required heavy grinding
  • Barrel shaped rib cage: indicates a shorter digestive tract, which is only viable on an energy rich diet
  • Larger brain: brain tissue is metabolically expensive, so a diet including cooked food and animal protein is the standard explanation for how a 1000 cm³ brain could be supported

Key fossil sites

Nariokotome, West Turkana, Kenya

Site of KNM-WT 15000, 'Turkana Boy', a nearly complete skeleton of an adolescent male discovered in 1984 and dated to about 1.6 million years ago.

The most complete early hominin skeleton ever found. It provides almost all of what is known about H. erectus body proportions and stature.

Koobi Fora, Kenya

Site of KNM-ER 3733, a well preserved cranium from the Turkana Basin dated to about 1.75 million years ago.

Shows the classic long low braincase and heavy brow ridge. Found in deposits that also contain P. boisei, evidence that the two species coexisted.

Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania

Site of OH 9, a large braincase with a very heavy brow ridge, dated to about 1.4 million years ago.

Found in a sequence that also yields Acheulean tools, linking the species to its tool culture in the same deposits.

Trinil and Sangiran, Java, Indonesia

Trinil 2, 'Java Man', is the type specimen, found by Eugène Dubois in 1891. Sangiran has yielded a large series of crania.

The first fossil hominin discovered outside Europe, and the first evidence that hominins had left Africa.

Zhoukoudian, China

A cave site near Beijing yielding 'Peking Man', a large assemblage of individuals dated between about 750 000 and 400 000 years ago.

Associated with ash layers and burnt bone, the classic evidence for the controlled use of fire.

Dmanisi, Georgia

A series of crania dated to about 1.8 million years ago.

The earliest hominin fossils known outside Africa, showing that dispersal began very soon after the species appeared.

Dating method

Potassium-Argon Dating

East African sites such as Nariokotome, Koobi Fora and Olduvai Gorge have volcanic ash layers that allow potassium-argon dating. The method is based on the decay of potassium-40 to argon-40 and calcium, with a half life of about 1.25 billion years, and it dates the volcanic rock above and below a fossil rather than the fossil itself.

Sites outside East Africa

The Javanese and Chinese sites lack suitable volcanic layers, so they are placed in sequence using relative methods such as stratigraphy, palaeomagnetism and faunal correlation.

★ Exam Focus
1Potassium-argon dating is used for East African sites. Say that it dates the volcanic rock, not the bone.
2Radiocarbon dating cannot be used, as even the youngest H. erectus fossils are far older than the 60 000 year limit of the method.
3Potassium-argon dating can only be used on material older than about 100 000 years, so it suits every H. erectus site that has volcanic layers.

Tool culture

Homo erectus is associated with the Acheulean tool culture, named after the site of Saint Acheul in northern France. The Acheulean appears at about 1.76 million years ago, follows the Oldowan, and persists until roughly 100 000 years ago, making it the longest lasting tool tradition in human history.

Acheulean tools were made by striking flakes from both faces of a core, which is called bifacial flaking, until a symmetrical tool was produced. Hard hammer percussion with a hammerstone removed the bulk of the material, then soft hammer percussion using bone, antler or wood produced finer finishing flakes and a straighter cutting edge. Because the maker works towards a predetermined shape, the Acheulean implies a mental template held in mind before the work begins.

The key tools are:

  • Handaxe: the signature Acheulean tool, teardrop or pear shaped, bifacially flaked, symmetrical, pointed at one end and rounded at the other
  • Cleaver: a large bifacial tool with a broad straight cutting edge rather than a point, used for heavy butchery
  • Pick: a thick, elongated, pointed tool used for digging and heavy breaking work
  • Flakes: the pieces struck off during manufacture, themselves used as sharp cutting and scraping tools

Hominins used Acheulean tools to butcher large animals, cut meat and hide, dig for roots and tubers, work wood, and break open bones for marrow.

Acheulean compared with Oldowan

Oldowan, H. habilis
  • Appears about 2.6 mya
  • One or two flakes struck from one face of a pebble
  • Irregular, with no standard form
  • Small pebble tools
  • Stone only
Acheulean, H. erectus
  • Appears about 1.76 mya
  • Flaked over both faces and around the whole circumference
  • Standardised, symmetrical, teardrop shaped
  • Larger and more robust
  • Stone, with bone, antler or wood used as hammers

Fire, home bases and social life

  • Controlled use of fire: the earliest widely accepted evidence, notably the ash and burnt bone layers at Zhoukoudian. Fire gave warmth, protection from predators, light that extended the working day, and the ability to cook
  • Cooking: supports both the reduction in tooth and jaw size and the increase in brain size
  • Home bases: repeated occupation of the same sites, with tools and animal bone accumulating together, suggests groups returned to fixed camps
  • Cooperation: hunting large animals and maintaining fire require coordination, which implies improved communication
  • Migration: spreading from Africa into Asia and Europe implies the ability to adapt behaviour, clothing and shelter to unfamiliar and colder environments
★ Exam Focus
1The tool culture of H. erectus is the Acheulean. Both spellings, Acheulean and Acheulian, appear in examination papers and both are accepted.
2Four features identify a tool as Acheulean: bifacial flaking or flaked on both sides, symmetrical cutting tools, handaxes with a teardrop shape, and larger or more robust tools.
3Learn the correct sequence of tool cultures: pebble tools, bifacial handaxes, flake tools, blades. That is Oldowan, Acheulean, Mousterian, Upper Palaeolithic.
4Do not attribute the Levallois method to H. erectus. Levallois is Mousterian and belongs to H. neanderthalensis.
5Trends across the tool cultures are a greater variety of materials, a greater number of blows and greater workmanship, and increasing specialisation including hafting. Each trend must be paired with a lifestyle implication to earn full marks.
