What is a tool culture?

A tool culture is a set of stone and other tools that share a way of being made, a range of shapes and a range of uses, and that is found with a particular hominin species. Four tool cultures are named in the course, one for each of the four Homo species that made them.

The syllabus asks for the trends across these four cultures rather than for each one in isolation. Its wording is that tool use "provides important insight into the evolution of the human cognitive abilities and lifestyles", and that trends are seen "in the changes in manufacturing techniques and the materials used". Everything on this page is arranged around those two things: what the tools were made of, and how they were made.

A tool is any object used to perform a task. An artefact is the word for a human made object found with the remains of a Homo species. The 2021 examination asked for that term specifically.

Before the tool cultures

The earliest tool use was not manufacture. Australopithecines are thought to have picked up sticks and stones from the local environment and used them as they were, without shaping them. Objects used in that way leave no archaeological trace, because an unmodified stone looks exactly like every other stone.

This is why the first tool culture belongs to Homo habilis. It is the first hominin known to have produced tools rather than simply used them.

Do not say australopithecines made Oldowan tools. The 2021 examination stem states the position clearly: the earliest use of tools would have been by australopithecines using sticks and stones picked up from the local environment, whereas Homo habilis is the first hominin known to have produced tools.

The sequence

The four cultures run in a fixed order, and the order is examinable. Each culture begins with the species named beside it, but cultures overlap in time and do not stop the moment the next one starts.

The signature tool of each culture. Pebble tool, bifacial hand axe, flake point, blade.

Stated as tool types rather than culture names, which is how the multiple choice questions put it, the order is pebble tools, bifacial hand axes, flake tools, blades.

The three trends

Three trends run across the four cultures. Each one has a consequence for the way hominins lived, and questions on trends almost always ask for the trend and what it tells us about lifestyle. One mark each. Learn them as pairs.

Trend, materials

A greater variety of materials is used, from stone only to bone, antler, ivory and wood.

What it tells us

Hominins used resources more effectively, used a wider variety of resources, and understood how to use their environment better.

Trend, workmanship

A greater number of blows is used, with increased workmanship, more time put into production and greater complexity.

What it tells us

Hominins completed more complex jobs, did finer work, and had time available to create.

Trend, specialisation

Tools became more specialised, tools were used to make other tools, and tools were hafted onto handles and shafts.

What it tells us

Hominins had more specialised roles within their communities.

A fourth way of putting the same point, used in 2024, is that early sets of tools are generic, meaning one shape does every job, while later sets show variety, many different types of tool for different jobs. A variety of tools implies a variety of roles.

The Upper Palaeolithic: one culture or three?

The tools of Homo sapiens are grouped together as the Upper Palaeolithic, and within that period three named cultures follow one another: the Aurignacian, then the Solutrean, then the Magdalenian. They share a species, a reliance on blade tools and a wide use of bone, antler and ivory, and they differ in the point styles and the specialised equipment that define each one.

Because they share so much, examination questions about H. sapiens tools are usually answered from the shared picture. The uses and lifestyle marks below are credited for Upper Palaeolithic tools as a whole rather than for one of the three cultures.

Upper Palaeolithic tool types, in stone, bone and antler, some hafted onto wooden shafts.

Six uses are credited, each paired with what it implies. Needles for sewing means they wore clothes. Weapons for hunting means they ate meat. Barbed spears and arrowheads attached to wood means spear hunting. Harpoons means fishing. Ornamental pieces and carvings on tools means art, figurines, jewellery, story telling and ritual beliefs. Burin tools used to make other tools means the use of alternative tool materials such as bone, ivory and antler.

Tools and the brain

The link between tools and cognitive ability is made at the level of the hominin group rather than for any one species. Bipedalism freed the hands to carry infants, tools and food. Manufacturing a tool requires mental dexterity and problem solving ability, and tools became more complex in their size, manufacture and use. Efficient tools meant a better ability to hunt for meat, and the increase in protein provided the energy required to support a larger brain.

A larger brain may in turn have contributed to more cooperation and communication, and speech and language are thought to have evolved as cranial capacity increased.

The Oldowan

The Oldowan is the first tool culture, made by Homo habilis. Its tools are also called pebble tools, and either name is accepted. They are the earliest manufactured stone tools known, first found at Olduvai Gorge, from which the culture takes its name.

The whole culture is made of stone only. Nothing in the Oldowan is made of bone, antler, ivory or wood, and nothing is hafted onto a handle.

An Oldowan core, a chopper with a flaked working edge, and a struck flake. Both the core and the flake were used as tools.

How they were made

One stone was struck against another to chip off one or two pieces, leaving a sharp cutting edge. This is simple percussion, and it is the least worked of the four techniques.

Both parts were used. The core, the stone that pieces were struck from, became a chopper with a jagged working edge. The flakes that came off were themselves sharp and were used for cutting.

The 2024 examination gave the model wording: simple percussion, whereby stones were struck against another to produce flakes. The wrong options in that question were the techniques of the three later cultures, so knowing what the Oldowan is not is as useful as knowing what it is.

Uses and what they tell us

Four uses are credited, and no others. Learn all four, because a question may ask for two.

  • Chopping up meat, butchering and skinning carcasses.
  • Digging up edible roots and plants.
  • Crushing and grinding tough plant material.
  • Breaking open animal bones, to reach the marrow.

Taken together these say that H. habilis was processing both meat and plant food, and was getting at food that an animal without tools could not reach. Bone marrow in particular is only available to something that can break a bone open.

The Acheulean

The Acheulean is the tool culture of Homo erectus, and it follows immediately after the Oldowan. Its defining tool is the hand axe, a large teardrop shaped tool worked on both faces.

It is still a stone only culture. What changes from the Oldowan is not the material but the amount of work: an Acheulean hand axe is shaped deliberately to a symmetrical form, which takes many more blows and requires the maker to hold an intended shape in mind before starting.

The Acheulean hand axe, teardrop shaped and flaked on both faces, and the cleaver, which has a broad straight cutting edge.

How they were made

A core is worked on both sides, which is what bifacial means, removing flakes all the way around until the tool is symmetrical. The result is larger and more robust than anything in the Oldowan, and the same tool serves as an all purpose cutting, chopping and butchering implement.

Four features identify an Acheulean tool, and a question may ask for two of them.

  • It shows bifacial flaking, meaning it is flaked on both sides.
  • It is a symmetrical cutting tool.
  • It is a hand axe, teardrop in shape.
  • It is larger and more robust than earlier tools.

Uses and what they tell us

No use has ever been credited for an Acheulean tool in a marking key, which is worth knowing so that you are not caught out expecting one. The hand axe is a general purpose tool, and the uses credited for hand axes elsewhere are chopping animal meat and making other tools.

What the Acheulean does show is planning. A symmetrical bifacial tool cannot be made by chance. The maker has to work the stone towards a shape that does not exist until the last flake comes off, which is a considerably more demanding task than striking one flake from a pebble.

The Mousterian

The Mousterian is the tool culture of Homo neanderthalensis. It is also accepted under the name flake tools, because its tools are made from flakes struck off a prepared core rather than from the core itself.

Two things change at this point in the sequence. Materials widen beyond stone for the first time, to bone, ivory and antler, and later keys also credit teeth and shells. And tools begin to be hafted, fitted to handles and shafts, which is what makes a thrusting spear possible.

A prepared Levallois core, a retouched point, a side scraper, and a point hafted onto a wooden shaft.

How they were made: the Levallois method

The Mousterian is the first culture in which the shape of the finished tool is decided before the flake is struck. The core is prepared first, so that a single blow releases a flake of a predetermined shape. Four steps carry marks.

  • Start with a large core or block of stone.
  • Prepare or work the core first, shaping its surface.
  • Strike large oval flakes from the worked core, by the percussion method. This is the Levallois technique.
  • Trim the edges by flaking off pieces around the outline of the flake. Pressure flaking may be used for the finer work.

Levallois is the one technique name other than percussion that the marking keys use. Naming it is worth a mark on its own, but only if you also describe the preparation of the core, because that is what the method actually means.

Tool types

Seven types are credited. A question asking you to describe what Neanderthal tools looked like will want the culture name and any three of them.

  • Flakes with reworked or sharpened edges.
  • Blade tools.
  • Core and scraper tools.
  • Hand axes.
  • Some with handles, that is, hafted.
  • Spear points.
  • Made of bone, ivory or antler.

The 2025 key adds knives and burins to this list, and gives the materials as teeth, shells and bones.

Uses and what they tell us

The Mousterian is the culture where uses start to be specific to tool types rather than general. Each pairing below is credited.

  • Spears and hafted points, to hunt larger animals. This means meat eating and organised hunting.
  • Scrapers and knives, to prepare animal hides. This means clothing.
  • Scrapers, blades and hand axes, to remove meat from animal bones and to build shelters.
  • Burins, to engrave and carve materials and to produce other tools.
  • A wider range of materials, teeth, shells and bones, to make more specialised tools.

The conclusion drawn from all of this is that Neanderthals had specialised roles within their communities, because a set of different tools for different jobs implies different people doing different jobs.

The Aurignacian

The Aurignacian, the Solutrean and the Magdalenian are the three tool cultures of Homo sapiens, and together they make up the Upper Palaeolithic. All three are built on the blade, a long narrow flake struck from a prepared core, and all three use bone, antler and ivory alongside stone.

The Aurignacian is the earliest of the three. It is the first tool culture of modern humans in Europe, and it appears while H. neanderthalensis is still present, so Mousterian and Aurignacian tools overlap in time.

Its defining change is the shift from the flake to the blade. A blade is at least twice as long as it is wide, and blades are struck one after another from a single prepared core, so one core yields many usable cutting edges. This is a far more economical use of stone than anything before it.

Aurignacian blade, burin with its angled chisel tip, and a bone point.

What is distinctive

  • Blade tools struck in series from a prepared core.
  • The burin, a blade with a narrow angled chisel edge, used to engrave and to work bone and antler.
  • End scrapers, worked at the end of a blade rather than along its side.
  • Bone and antler points, the first widespread use of these materials for weapon tips.
  • The earliest figurative art and personal ornament, including carved figures and beads.

The burin matters more than its size suggests. It is a tool for making other tools, and it is what makes systematic working of bone and antler possible. Every later development in bone technology depends on it.

Uses and what they tell us

The Aurignacian shares the six credited Upper Palaeolithic uses set out on the Overview tab. What is particular to it is the beginning of two of them: carving and ornament, which imply art and ritual belief, and the burin, which implies the use of alternative tool materials.

The Solutrean

The Aurignacian, the Solutrean and the Magdalenian are the three tool cultures of Homo sapiens, and together they make up the Upper Palaeolithic. All three are built on the blade, a long narrow flake struck from a prepared core, and all three use bone, antler and ivory alongside stone.

The Solutrean is the middle of the three, and it is the one the examinations have actually tested. It is known for finely worked bifacial points, of which the best known is the laurel leaf, a slender leaf shaped blade worked over both faces until it is thin, symmetrical and sharp all round.

The Solutrean laurel leaf point, a blade, and an eyed bone needle.

How they were made: pressure flaking

The laurel leaf is not made by striking. It is finished by pressure flaking, in which a pointed tool of bone or antler is pressed against the edge of the stone to remove a small flake, rather than hitting it. Pressing gives far more control than striking, so the maker can take off very small flakes exactly where they are wanted.

The result is the thinnest and most precisely shaped stone tool in the whole sequence. Some laurel leaves are so thin and so regular that they are thought to have been made to be looked at rather than used, which if true would be the clearest evidence in any tool culture of work done for its own sake.

Pressure flaking is credited in the marking keys, in 2018 and 2020, although in both cases in a Mousterian context. It is the one technique term you can use for the Solutrean that a marker will already recognise.

What is distinctive

  • Laurel leaf and willow leaf points, bifacially worked and very thin.
  • Pressure flaking as the finishing technique.
  • Shouldered points, with a notch at the base for hafting.
  • Eyed bone needles, which appear in western Europe from this period, implying sewn and fitted clothing.

The Magdalenian

The Aurignacian, the Solutrean and the Magdalenian are the three tool cultures of Homo sapiens, and together they make up the Upper Palaeolithic. All three are built on the blade, a long narrow flake struck from a prepared core, and all three use bone, antler and ivory alongside stone.

The Magdalenian is the most recent of the three and the last tool culture in the course. It is the point at which every trend in the sequence has run its full course: the widest range of materials, the greatest workmanship, and the most specialised tools.

Stone blades become small and standardised, and much of the interesting work moves into bone and antler. The barbed harpoon, carved from antler and lashed to a shaft, is its signature tool.

Magdalenian barbed antler harpoon, eyed bone needle, hafted point and blade.

What is distinctive

  • Barbed harpoons of antler and bone, for fishing and for hunting.
  • Small standardised blades, set into handles of bone or wood.
  • Eyed needles, in wide use, implying tailored clothing.
  • The spear thrower, a shaft that extends the arm and throws a spear much further.
  • The great painted caves, which belong to this period.

The spear thrower and the harpoon are both worth noticing for the same reason: neither is a tool that does a job directly. Each is a device for making a person better at a job, which is a further step again from a hand axe that simply cuts.

Uses and what they tell us

All six of the credited Upper Palaeolithic lifestyle inferences are visible in the Magdalenian at once. Needles mean clothing. Weapons mean meat eating. Barbed spears and arrowheads attached to wood mean spear hunting. Harpoons mean fishing. Carvings and ornament mean art, figurines, jewellery, story telling and ritual belief. Burins used to make other tools mean the use of bone, ivory and antler.

Fishing is worth singling out, because it is a food source that no earlier culture could reach. A barbed harpoon is the first tool in the sequence that opens up an entirely new part of the environment.
